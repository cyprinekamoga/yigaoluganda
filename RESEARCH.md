## 1. Luganda basics that affect lesson design

### 1.1 Spelling is (almost) phonemic
- Luganda is written in the Latin alphabet, and letters map to sounds very consistently. Children who can read Swedish or English can usually "sound out" Luganda.
- The special letter **ŋ** (as in *ŋŋenda*, "I go") is often typed as **ng'** on keyboards without ŋ. **Answer checking must treat `ng'` and `ŋ` as the same letter.**
- **ny** is one sound (as in Spanish *ñ*): *nnyabo* ("madam"), *nnya* ("four").
- Luganda has five vowels: a, e, i, o, u. They are pronounced as in Swedish, not as in English. That's an advantage for Swedish-track learners.

### 1.2 Length matters: double vowels and double consonants
- Vowel length and consonant length (gemination) **change meaning**. Doubled letters are spelled out: *aa, ee, ii, oo, uu* and *bb, dd, gg, kk, mm, nn, ss, tt, zz, …*
- Textbooks teach minimal pairs where only the length differs. We deliberately don't list examples here until a native speaker supplies verified ones (**NEEDS NATIVE SPEAKER CHECK**). The point for the app: **answer checking must never collapse double letters into single ones.** *amazi* typed for *amazzi* ("water") is a different spelling and must not be accepted silently.
- Design decision: when a typed answer is wrong *only* because of a doubled/single letter, the app shows a gentle, specific hint ("So close! Listen for the double letters") rather than a generic "wrong". It still counts as not-yet-correct, so the word comes back in practice.
- Tone exists in Luganda but is not written in standard spelling, so it can't be taught through text. That's one more reason audio recordings by native speakers are essential (see §3).

### 1.3 Noun classes
Like other Bantu languages, Luganda sorts nouns into classes. Each class has a prefix, usually with an initial vowel (the "augment"). Common singular/plural pairs:

| Class pair | Singular → plural | Example (meaning) |
|---|---|---|
| 1/2 (people) | **omu-** → **aba-** | omwana → abaana (child → children), omusomesa → abasomesa (teacher → teachers) |
| 3/4 | **omu-** → **emi-** | omukono → emikono (arm → arms) |
| 5/6 | **e(ri)-/eC-** → **ama-** | eriiso → amaaso (eye → eyes) |
| 7/8 (things) | **eki-** → **ebi-** | ekitabo → ebitabo (book → books) |
| 9/10 | **en-/em-** → **en-/em-** | embwa → embwa (dog → dogs) |
| 11 | **olu-** | oluggi (door) |
| 12/14 (small things) | **aka-** → **obu-** | akamwa (mouth) |
| 15 | **oku-** | okutu (ear), also all infinitives: okulya (to eat) |

What this means for teaching:
1. **Words agree with their noun.** Adjectives, numbers, possessives and verb subjects change prefix with the noun's class. *My* is *wange* in *maama wange* (my mother) but *kyange* in *ekitabo kyange* (my book). So we **teach possessives and adjectives as whole chunks** ("maama wange", "ekitabo kyange"), never as a separate "my" tile that can be glued onto anything.
2. **Plurals aren't "add -s".** Plurals are taught as separate vocabulary items or as pairs (omwana / abaana), not generated by code.
3. **Numbers agree too.** *Bbiri* (two) in counting becomes *babiri* with people (abaana babiri). Lessons teach counting forms first and flag any agreed forms.
4. **The augment (initial vowel) drops** in some contexts (after negatives, in some names, in vocative use). That's why "the same word" can appear as *amazzi* or *mazzi*. Content stores the exact form used in each sentence, not a computed one.

### 1.4 Verbs are built from parts
A single Luganda word can be a whole English sentence: *Ndya* = "I eat", *Tulye!* = "Let's eat!". Subject, tense and object are prefixes and suffixes on the verb.
- **Consequence:** sentence-building tiles cannot map one Luganda word to one English word. Tile exercises present **Luganda tiles only** and ask the child to rebuild a Luganda sentence from its meaning. They never ask the child to align word by word.
- Infinitives start with **oku-/okw-**: okulya (to eat), okwagala (to love/want), okuzannya (to play). They're good first verb forms because they don't change.

### 1.5 Why word-by-word translation fails
- **Greetings are questions about how you spent your time**, not "hello": *Wasuze otya?* is literally "How did you spend the night?" ≈ "Good morning". *Osiibye otya?* ≈ "How has your day been?" (afternoon/evening).
- ***Gyebale ko*** acknowledges someone's work or effort, so it has no direct English/Swedish equivalent ("well done / thanks for your work / hello").
- **One word can be a whole sentence** (see 1.4), and many English words have no single equivalent. For example, Luganda kinship words like *ssenga* (father's sister) and *kojja* (mother's brother) are more specific than "aunt" and "uncle".
- **Design decision:** every phrase is stored with a *natural* English and Swedish meaning, plus an optional `literal` field and a `note` for culture. The UI shows the natural meaning; the literal meaning is a "did you know?" extra.

### 1.6 Respect, greetings and kneeling culture
- Greeting is important in Buganda culture. Skipping it is rude. A greeting is often a short exchange (question → answer → return question).
- **Elders are greeted with respect terms**: *ssebo* (sir) and *nnyabo* (madam), and often the emphatic *nno* (*Wasuze otya nno, nnyabo?*).
- **Kneeling (okufukamira):** traditionally, girls and women kneel when greeting elders and parents, and boys may bow or crouch. Practice varies by family, generation and setting, and it's discussed and debated today. The app presents this **neutrally as cultural knowledge** ("In many Baganda families, children kneel to greet elders"). It doesn't prescribe it, and it doesn't differentiate by gender in exercises.
- Children in the diaspora often meet Luganda through grandparents (*jjajja*), so greeting elders is a core early unit.

## 2. How Duolingo-style apps teach (and what we copy or avoid)

### 2.1 Structure
- **Short lessons** take about 3–5 minutes and 10–15 exercises. For 6–12 year olds we aim for **8–12 exercises**, roughly 3 minutes.
- **A linear path of units.** Each unit is a theme (greetings, family…). Units contain 3–5 lessons, and finishing one unlocks the next. A linear path removes choice paralysis for kids.
- **New material is mixed with review.** Each lesson introduces about 3–5 new words and recycles earlier ones.
- **Varied exercise types** keep attention up: recognition (multiple choice, picture match, match pairs, listening) comes before production (tile building, fill-in, typing). Recognition is easier, so it comes earlier in each lesson and earlier in the course.

### 2.2 Spaced repetition
- The spacing effect (Ebbinghaus; many later replications) shows that reviewing material at increasing intervals beats massed practice.
- Duolingo's published model is half-life regression (Settles & Meeder, ACL 2016). It estimates each word's memory "half-life" from a learner's history.
- For an MVP with no server, a **Leitner box system** is simpler, transparent and good enough. Each word sits in box 0–5. A correct answer moves it up one box; a wrong answer moves it back to box 1. Review intervals are 0, 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 days. "Practice" picks words that are due, weakest first.

### 2.3 Gamification that motivates without dark patterns
Kids are more vulnerable to manipulative design. Guidance such as the UK ICO's *Age Appropriate Design Code* (a UK code, but a useful benchmark for EU products) warns against "nudge techniques" that push children to stay longer. Our choices:

| Mechanic | Use it? | How we keep it healthy |
|---|---|---|
| XP | ✅ | Rewards finishing and accuracy. No leaderboards against strangers. |
| Streak | ✅ | Celebrated when kept. When broken, it just restarts. There are **no guilt messages, no push notifications, no paid "streak freeze"**. |
| Hearts | ✅ (gentle) | Mistakes cost a heart, but hearts **refill over time** (1 per 20 min) and **fully refill after a Practice round**, so a child is never stuck. There's no payment and no ads. |
| Badges | ✅ | For effort and milestones (first lesson, 3-day streak, finishing a story). |
| Timers / countdowns | ❌ | They cause stress for young kids. |
| Loot boxes, purchases, ads | ❌ | None in the app at all. |
| Social comparison | ❌ | None. |

### 2.4 Feedback
- Correct answers get an immediate, short celebration: an animated mascot, a colour flash and an optional sound effect (off for reduced motion).
- Wrong answers get a **gentle correction that always shows the right answer**. There's no red "FAIL" and no buzzer. The wording is encouraging ("Almost! The answer is…").
- The wrong item comes back later in the same lesson, which is the standard "retry at the end" pattern.

## 3. Good learning UI for 6–12 year olds

- **Big tap targets.** WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 requires at least 24×24 CSS px, and SC 2.5.5 (AAA) asks for 44×44. Children's motor control is less precise, so we use **at least 56 px high buttons and 64+ px answer cards**, with generous spacing.
- **Little reading.** Instructions are short ("Tap the right picture") and paired with icons and emoji pictures. Younger children (6–7) may not read fluently, so pictures carry meaning wherever possible.
- **Audio first, where we have it.** Native-speaker recordings are the gold standard for Luganda because tone isn't written. **Text-to-speech is not used for Luganda**, since generic TTS voices mispronounce it and teach wrong habits. The speaker button only appears when a real recording exists.
- **Positive feedback, predictable layout.** The primary button is always at the bottom in the same place, and the mascot is always the "voice" of feedback.
- **Accessibility:**
  - Colour contrast at WCAG AA (4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text and UI parts). Colour is never the only signal; correct/incorrect states also use an icon and text.
  - `prefers-reduced-motion` turns off non-essential animation.
  - Full keyboard support, visible focus rings, semantic buttons, and `aria-live` announcements of feedback for screen readers.
  - `lang="lg"` on Luganda text so assistive tech doesn't try to read it as English or Swedish.
  - A rounded, highly legible font with large sizes (body ≥ 18 px).

## 4. Privacy: children's apps in Sweden/EU (GDPR)

- **GDPR** (Regulation 2016/679) applies to any processing of personal data about people in the EU.
- **Article 8 / Swedish implementation:** where consent is the legal basis for an information society service offered directly to a child, Sweden sets the age at **13** (Dataskyddslagen 2018:218). Below that, parental consent is required. Our users are 6–12, so any data collection would need verifiable parental consent.
- **Data minimisation and privacy by design** (Art. 5(1)(c), Art. 25) mean we should collect only what we need, which here is nothing.
- **The ePrivacy rules** (cookie rules; Swedish *Lag om elektronisk kommunikation*) allow storing information on a device without consent only when it's *strictly necessary* for the service the user asked for. Saving a child's own lesson progress locally falls within that. Analytics or advertising cookies would not.
- **IMY** (Integritetsskyddsmyndigheten) is the Swedish supervisory authority.

**Decisions for the MVP:**
1. **No accounts, no logins, no server, no analytics, no third-party trackers, no ads.**
2. Progress is stored **only on the device** in `localStorage`. Nothing leaves the device.
3. The only "personal" item is the mascot's name, which the child chooses. It isn't the child's name, and the UI says so.
4. Fonts are **self-hosted** (bundled with the app), so no Google Fonts request leaks IP addresses.
5. The parent page explains this and offers a one-tap "erase all progress on this device".
6. If sync or accounts are added later, that requires a DPIA, parental consent flows and a privacy notice written for children. This is out of scope for the MVP.
